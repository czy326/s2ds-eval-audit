## Figure 1

**Caption.**
Seed-to-seed variability of the per-image PSNR decreases as the test set grows.
Each marker is one benchmark. The horizontal axis is the number of test images
on a logarithmic scale and the vertical axis is the median seed-to-seed standard
deviation of the paired per-image PSNR difference, in dB. Error bars span the
minimum to the maximum standard deviation observed for that benchmark. The four
points are AID (1000 images, median 0.0728 dB), RSSCN7 (281 images, 0.0632 dB),
UCMerced (210 images, 0.1070 dB), and WHU-RS19 (101 images, 0.1202 dB). The
relation is monotone in test-set size except for the inversion between AID and
RSSCN7, which we attribute to the differing content heterogeneity of the two
benchmarks rather than to size alone. The practical reading is that a fixed
absolute effect, such as the 0.1 dB that RS-SR papers commonly claim, is compared
against a smaller noise floor on a large benchmark and a larger one on a small
benchmark.

**Source.** `results/audit_C_seednoise.json`, aggregated per dataset.
**File.** `figures/fig1_noise_vs_testsize.{png,pdf,svg}`, 300 dpi.

## Figure 2

**Caption.**
Method effect against seed noise for 327 method pairs. Each point is one pair of
runs on the same dataset sharing a seed. The horizontal axis is the absolute
mean paired PSNR difference in dB and the vertical axis is the seed-to-seed
standard deviation of that difference in dB. Both axes are logarithmic and a
dashed line marks equality. Points above the line have an effect larger than the
noise. Points below the line, colored, are the 172 pairs (52.6%) whose difference
does not clear the noise floor under the reference-method definition. Of the 327
pairs, 83 (25.4%) reverse sign across seeds. The cluster of points near the lower-left corner corresponds to pairs on
AID between method variants that differ by a single component, where both the
effect and the noise are of order 0.01 dB. The figure makes the central quantity
of the audit visible: the vertical distance from the equality line is the margin
by which a claimed improvement exceeds what a rerun would reproduce.

**Source.** `results/audit_D_effect_vs_noise.json`, 327 pair records.
**File.** `figures/fig2_effect_vs_noise.{png,pdf,svg}`, 300 dpi.

## Table 1

**Caption.**
Adjacent-pair comparison in SpectralSR-Bench, a 2026 evaluation paper that
reports `mean ± std` in every table. Consecutive rows of the paper's ranking are
paired, and the difference in mean PSNR is compared against the larger of the two
reported standard deviations. In 8 of the 10 adjacent pairs the difference in
means is smaller than the reported dispersion, so the ranking is not resolvable
from the reported statistics despite the dispersion being disclosed. The two
pairs that do clear the dispersion involve a large change of operating regime
(a three-step cascade and a diffusion method near failure). The `std` column is

table supports the weaker claim that the method differences are smaller than the
data dispersion, and it is the only case in the survey where the required

## Table 2

**Caption.**
Rank correlation against the reference convention for eight aggregation and
metric conventions, on four benchmarks. The reference is mean PSNR. Each cell
gives the Spearman rank correlation between the method ranking under that
convention and the ranking under mean PSNR, and a cross marks a convention under
which the top-ranked method differs from the mean-PSNR champion. Median
aggregation changes the champion on all four datasets, with the correlation
falling to 0.502 on RSSCN7. The 10% and 25% trimmed means preserve the ranking
on every dataset, with correlations of at least 0.833, and mostly above 0.93.
The contrast between median and trimmed mean localizes the instability to the
tails and the immediate neighborhood of the center rather than to the overall
shape of the distribution. The single lowest correlation in the study is 0.272,
for the SAM convention on WHU-RS19. That case is also the only one in which the
changing champion belongs to a different method family, a residual-attention
network under mean PSNR and a lightweight CNN under SAM, and it occurs on the
smallest benchmark at 101 test images.

**Source.** `results/audit_B_protocols.json`.

## Table 3

**Caption.**
Effect size against seed noise across 327 method pairs. A pair is two runs on the
same dataset that share a seed and belong to different methods. For each pair we
record the mean paired per-image PSNR difference, the seed-to-seed standard
deviation of that difference for each method, and whether the sign of the
difference is the same across all seeds. Of 327 pairs, 83 (25.4%) reverse sign
between seeds. The fraction whose mean effect is smaller than the noise depends
on which standard deviation the effect is compared against, and the caption
states all three. Against the smaller of the two methods' standard deviations,
154 pairs (47.1%) fall below the noise. Against the reference method's standard
deviation, 172 pairs (52.6%) fall below, and this is the number used elsewhere in
the paper. Against the larger of the two, 224 pairs (68.5%) fall below. The
median seed-to-seed standard deviation is 0.088 dB and the median method effect is
between 0.018 and 0.039 dB, so the noise is two to five times the effect it is
meant to discriminate. The reversal rate counts pairs whose ranking is not
reproducible across seeds at the level of a single rerun.

**Source.** `results/audit_D_effect_vs_noise.json`.

## Table 4

**Caption.**
Seed noise and reversal rate by benchmark. Columns give the number of test
images, the median and maximum seed-to-seed standard deviation of the paired
per-image PSNR difference in dB, and the fraction of pairs whose sign reverses
across seeds. The median standard deviation is smallest on AID at 1000 images
and largest on WHU-RS19 at 101 images. WHU-RS19 also has the highest reversal
rate, 47.3%, and 80% of its pairs fall below the noise. The ordering of the
median standard deviation is monotone in test-set size. A consequence for
practice is that a 0.1 dB claim on WHU-RS19 lies below the median seed variation
of that benchmark.

**Source.** `results/audit_C_seednoise.json`.

## Table 5

**Caption.**
Report practice across 35 remote-sensing super-resolution and evaluation papers,
mainly from 2026. Each paper is coded on whether it states a verifiable specific
dB gain, provides a code or data link, mentions training seeds or variance or a
statistical test in the abstract, states its metric-aggregation convention, and
anchors its evaluation to a downstream task or a protocol reproduction. Where
full texts were retrieved, the coding is repeated at the body level and reported
against the full sample of 35. Seven papers (20.0%) state a specific dB gain that
a reader could check, and 28 (80.0%) report an absolute PSNR, a qualitative
claim, or an efficiency comparison only. No abstract mentions seeds, variance, or
a statistical test. Four hundredths of the abstracts, 4 of 35, mention the
aggregation convention. The body-level counts, 8 of 35 for aggregation, 4 of 35
for seeds, and 15 of 35 for test-set composition, come from the 18 full texts and
are lower bounds on the true rates rather than estimates of them.

**Source.** `results/audit_F_field_survey.json`.

## Table 6

**Caption.**
Claimed gain against the measured seed-noise floor for the seven papers that
state a verifiable specific dB gain. For each paper the claimed effect is divided
by the maximum seed standard deviation measured on the same benchmark, where that
benchmark is in our pool, and the noise source is named in the third column.
Where the paper's benchmark is outside our pool, the division uses the pool-wide
median and the row is marked as a fallback. Two claims, PLAN at +0.07 dB and
JNUN-CA at +0.19 dB, fall below the noise ceiling. Two more, DBTSR at +0.12 dB
and IR275K at +0.35 dB, sit just above it. The three large claims, AstraMoE-SR at
+0.64 dB, DFSMamba at +1.07 dB, and EORestore-Agent at +2.30 dB, clear the noise
by a wide margin and are not in question. The pattern is concentrated in the
interval between 0.05 and 0.19 dB, which is where the common claim of beating the
state of the art by about 0.1 dB is made. Fallback rows cannot be read as the
noise on the paper's own dataset.

## Table 7

**Caption.**
The R1 to R7 reproducibility scorecard. Each row names a disclosure, the failure
that follows when it is omitted, and the status of that disclosure in the audit
pool. The checks are ordered by the cost of omission measured in this study. R2
carries the largest measured effect, because the aggregation convention changes
the champion on all four benchmarks. R3 and R4 together carry the reversal rate
and the noise floor, 25.4% and a median 0.088 dB. R5 carries the 5% shift that
comes from changing only the baseline endpoint convention. R1 is listed first
because it is a prerequisite: a comparison built on different test images is not
a comparison. The scorecard is designed to be applied to a published paper or a
competition report, producing a profile of which of the seven the work omits.

**Source.** Authored from the audit findings in Sections 4 and 5.

## Caption conventions used

- Figure text is English only. Table headers are English.
- Every caption states the unit of every axis or column.
- Every caption states the denominator explicitly ("of 327 pairs", "of 35 papers").
- Every caption that depends on a measured quantity names the fallback condition
  where one applies.
- No caption asserts that a paper fails to report something. Where a disclosure
  was not found, the caption says it was not found in the retrieved text.
