 Abstract Advances in satellite imaging have positioned remote sensing as a critical tool for resource management and urban planning. However, current systems grapple with sensor limitations and atmospheric interference that induce image defects such as blurred edges and missing textures, alongside computational inefficiencies and limited generalization of traditional super-resolution methods. This work proposes SDGAN (Super-Densely Connected Generative Adversarial Network), a lightweight framework integrating a super-dense residual module with 2D convolution kernels to enhance local feature representation and edge optimization while minimizing artifacts; a dual-discriminator system leveraging attention U-Net further improves efficiency by prioritizing critical features and reducing computational demands. Experiments on UCMerced-LandUse and WHU-RS19 datasets demonstrate state-of-the-art performance, achieving 21.72% and 14.47% runtime reductions over baselines, respectively, while effectively balancing reconstruction quality and computational efficiency in real-world remote sensing scenarios.

 Introduction Advances in high-resolution satellite imaging have significantly expanded the application of remote sensing in smart city development, disaster monitoring, and ecological assessment. However, sensor physical limitations and atmospheric propagation effects often result in raw images with insufficient spatial resolution and degraded texture details, which compromise their utility in land use classification and scene interpretation. In recent years, deep learning has become a core driving force in advancing remote sensing image processing, with its powerful feature learning capabilities enabling breakthroughs across multiple tasks. For instance, in landslide extraction from aerial imagery, models leveraging context-aware features (e.g., U-Net combined with attention mechanisms) capture spatial correlations between landslide areas and surrounding terrain (vegetation-covered zones, exposed rock regions), avoiding missing small-scale landslides and blurry boundary segmentation–some even achieve an F1-score of over 90% in mountain landslide extraction, providing precise spatial references for disaster emergency assessment.This context-aware feature fusion logic inspires our focus on edge and detail preservation in super-resolution. In hyperspectral anomaly detection, deep learning methods further demonstrate innovative value: Frequency-to-Spectrum Mapping GAN (FTSGAN) 1 realizes semi-supervised detection by mapping original spectra to the fractional Fourier domain, highlighting deep learning’s strength in cross-domain feature modeling for remote sensing data; Global Feature-Injected Blind-Spot Network 2 integrates global feature injection mechanism with blind-spot network structure to make up for the “blind spot” of single-scale feature extraction, reflecting the necessity of “global-local collaboration” in adapting to complex remote sensing scenarios. Notably, multi-aspect collaborative salient object detectors integrate “localization, balance, and affinity” mechanisms to enhance recognition robustness against scale variations and background interference. This framework lays a foundation for our “detail-structure collaborative recovery” strategy, guiding the dual discriminator design that balances small-scale texture and large-scale structure.In other key tasks, progress is equally notable: cross-view intelligent person search methods solve perspective mismatches via multi-feature constraint (appearance, posture, environment), offering inspiration for balancing local details and global structure in super-resolution; Wang et al. 3 propose a Cross-Domain Detection Transformer with multi-view adaptive feature alignment for remote sensing cross-domain object detection, verifying that multi-view adaptive feature alignment can effectively address ground object scale diversity; multi-aspect collaborative salient object detectors integrate “localization, balance, and affinity” mechanisms to enhance recognition robustness, laying a foundation for “detail-structure collaborative recovery” in subsequent super-resolution tasks. In the specific field of super-resolution (SR) reconstruction–critical for mitigating resolution limitations–deep learning methods (including convolutional neural networks, generative adversarial networks, and attention-based architectures) outperform traditional interpolation or sparse representation approaches by enabling end-to-end learning of complex spatial structures and texture dependencies in remote sensing images, effectively alleviating issues caused by sensor constraints and atmospheric interference.For remote sensing SR specifically, improved residual networks have been proposed to enhance reconstruction performance 4 . Furthermore, residual dense backprojection networks have been designed to mitigate detail loss in single remote sensing image SR 5 . Recent advances further confirm the potential of lightweight GAN frameworks and multi-scale attention mechanisms in remote sensing SR: Li et al. 6 proposed a lightweight GAN with cross-scale feature fusion, achieving improved efficiency but still struggling with artifact suppression in complex urban-suburban scenes; Wang et al. 7 emphasized the importance of SR for land-use classification, yet their multi-scale attention model failed to balance global structure and local detail restoration; Chen et al. 8 designed adaptive residual blocks for UAV-borne image SR, but the model’s generalization across diverse land-use categories remains limited. While deep learning-based SR has shown substantial progress in enhancing image quality, existing methodologies still face three critical challenges: (1) artifacts such as blurred regions and anomalous textures frequently emerge in complex scenes (e.g., mixed urban-suburban areas, dense vegetation), distorting land-object boundary information; (2) inadequate adaptation to multiscale features (e.g., small-scale road edges vs. large-scale river networks) limits generalization across diverse land-use categories; (3) the growing gap between network complexity and computational efficiency hinders real-time processing in resource-constrained scenarios (e.g., UAV-mounted devices for on-site disaster monitoring). Current deep learning-based approaches exhibit further key limitations: Since the proposal of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) 9 , GAN-based SR methods (e.g., SRGAN 10 , EEGAN 11 ) struggle with high-frequency detail reconstruction accuracy while generating artifacts; Inspired by recurrent visual attention models 12 , attention mechanisms (e.g., MGAN 13 , TAGAN 14 ) demonstrate enhanced feature extraction but fail to address remote sensing images’ multi-scale characteristics; and driven by the success of deep residual learning 15 , residual block architectures (e.g., RDN 16 , PMSRN 17 ) achieve improved feature extraction at the cost of exponential computational complexity increases with network deepening, impeding deployment in resource-constrained environments. To address the aforementioned pain points, this study proposes a lightweight remote sensing image super-resolution framework–SDGAN (Super-Densely Connected Generative Adversarial Network). Through two core innovations, it achieves collaborative optimization of detail recovery, generalization capability, and resource utilization. Its key contributions can be summarized as follows: 
 
 1. 
 
 Super-dense residual block (SDRB) for accurate detail reconstruction A super-dense residual block with 2D convolution kernels and diagonal connections is designed, establishing multi-dimensional connectivity patterns. Compared with traditional single-path skip connection architectures, it significantly improves inter-layer feature propagation and utilization efficiency, enabling more accurate capture and restoration of image details while effectively suppressing artifacts and optimizing edge performance. 
 
 
 
 2. 
 
 Attention U-net dual discriminator for efficient generalization An attention U-Net dual discriminator network is constructed based on the lightweight Lite-U-Net. By combining the attention mechanism with the hierarchical structure of U-Net, it reduces computational complexity while enhancing focus on image details and high-frequency components. Through multi-scale discrimination, it balances local details and global structure, significantly improving the model’s generalization capability and computational efficiency. 
 
 
 To verify the effectiveness of SDGAN, comprehensive experiments were conducted on three major remote sensing datasets: UCMerced-LandUse, WHU-RS19, and AID. The results show that the framework achieves runtime reductions of 21.72% and 14.47% on the UCMerced-LandUse and WHU-RS19 datasets, respectively. Meanwhile, it effectively balances reconstruction quality and computational efficiency in real-world remote sensing scenarios, providing a high-performance and lightweight new solution for remote sensing image super-resolution tasks in resource-constrained environments. The remainder of this paper is structured to elaborate on the research in a logical manner: First, the “Related Work” section will systematically review existing studies on GAN-based, attention mechanism-based, and residual block-based super-resolution methods, analyzing their limitations in remote sensing scenarios to further clarify the innovative positioning of SDGAN. Next, the “Methodology” section will detail the technical design of SDGAN, including the feature propagation mechanism of SDRB, the multi-scale discrimination logic of the dual Attention U-Net discriminator, and the formulation of the loss function tailored for remote sensing images. Then, the “Experiments” section will present in-depth validation: comparative experiments with mainstream baselines (e.g., SRGAN, Real-ESRGAN) and ablation experiments on the three datasets will be conducted, using both quantitative metrics (PSNR, SSIM, FID, NIQE) and qualitative visual analysis to demonstrate SDGAN’s superiority in reconstruction quality and computational efficiency. Finally, the “Conclusion and Future Work” section will summarize the key findings of this study and propose potential directions for further optimization, such as adapting SDGAN to complex image degradations (e.g., haze, motion blur) and exploring its deployment on edge computing devices for real-time remote sensing applications. Related work Driven by deep learning advancements, remote sensing image super-resolution (SR) research has focused on three core directions: GAN-based methods, attention mechanism-based approaches, and residual block-based architectures, with existing studies still facing gaps in artifact suppression, multi-scale adaptation, and computational efficiency. Meanwhile, deep learning progress in other remote sensing tasks (e.g., hyperspectral anomaly detection, cross-domain object detection) provides valuable technical references for addressing these SR limitations–this section integrates such insights to clarify this study’s innovation. GAN-based super-resolution reconstruction methods GAN-based SR methods excel in fine-grained detail generation via adversarial learning, but their application in remote sensing remains constrained by critical limitations. Early methods primarily optimized quantitative metrics like peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) 18 by minimizing mean squared error (MSE) between original and reconstructed images, while SRGAN 10 pioneered perceptual loss for quality enhancement yet lacked cross-domain generalization. Subsequent improvements–MRMGAN 19 ’s multi-resolution networks for better generalization, EnhanceNet 20 ’s texture-matching loss for realistic textures, SRFeat 21 ’s dual-discriminator design with image/feature-level adversarial losses, ESRGAN 22 ’s residual-dense blocks (RRDB) and pre-activation features for perceptual quality, and Real-ESRGAN 23 ’s high-order models for real-world degradation simulation–addressed specific flaws but failed to resolve remote sensing-specific issues. In remote sensing scenarios, Enlighten-GAN 24 improved visual quality via self-supervised hierarchical perceptual loss but risked texture over-smoothing; EEGAN 11 strengthened edge information for robust noise reconstruction but suffered from imbalanced architecture and poor non-edge texture recovery; SG-FBGAN 25 addressed region diversity via saliency-guided feedback connections at the cost of increased computational complexity; CDGANs 26 enhanced performance by jointly optimizing generated images and ground truths but faced training instability. Li et al. 6 proposed a lightweight GAN with cross-scale feature fusion for remote sensing SR, which reduces computational overhead by 18.2% compared to Real-ESRGAN but still generates noticeable artifacts in dense vegetation and intersecting road scenes due to insufficient multi-directional feature propagation. Collectively, GAN-based remote sensing SR methods still struggle with artifact generation due to over-optimization and insufficient consideration of model stability and computational efficiency–notably, they rarely draw on the “multi-scale balance” mechanism of remote sensing salient object detection (e.g., balancing small-target localization and large-scale background adaptation), leading to common problems of over-optimized local details and distorted global structures. Relevant hyperspectral anomaly detection studies offer solutions: FTSGAN 1 proves cross-domain feature modeling (via frequency-to-spectrum mapping) can make up for single spatial-domain learning deficiencies, and Global Feature-Injected Blind-Spot Network 2 verifies the value of global feature integration with blind-spot structure in remote sensing anomaly detection. Thus, SDGAN adopts SDRB for multi-dimensional feature connections (learning from FTSGAN’s cross-domain mapping logic) and a dual discriminator for local-global verification (referencing the global-local collaboration mechanism of 2 ), alleviating traditional SR-GAN flaws. Attention mechanism-based super-resolution methods Attention mechanisms, inspired by human visual systems, enhance SR performance by prioritizing critical features (e.g., textures, edges) while suppressing irrelevant information, yet their remote sensing application is limited by trade-offs between detail preservation, stability, and efficiency. MGAN 13 integrated multi-scale strategies with attention to refine feature responses and improve SR accuracy/computational efficiency but lacked adaptation to remote sensing’s multi-scale characteristics; CAL 27 used causal inference to assess attention quality and performed well across integer/non-integer scaling factors, dynamic attention 28 generated adaptive weights for input samples to enhance magnification capabilities, and GCA-LEA global-local dual attention 29 exploited inter-layer dependencies for high-frequency recovery–yet none addressed computational redundancy in remote sensing scenarios. To balance performance and resources, EMASRN 30 reduced parameters via specialized attention blocks while maintaining image quality, hybrid channel-spatial attention 31 improved detail restoration, but fusion with GANs introduced new flaws: attention-guided GANs 32 optimized textures but underperformed in high-noise/extreme-texture scenarios; Lu and Hu’s GAN 33 combined channel/spatial attention for better feature exchange but increased parameter complexity; GDCA 34 generated sharper outputs but required complexity reduction; attention-modified SRGAN 35 improved noise robustness but lacked cross-scenario adaptability; TAGAN 14 introduced gradient-weighted loss with attention for texture recovery but compromised global quality; Wang et al. 7 developed a multi-scale attention model tailored for SR-based land-use classification, which enhances texture detail preservation but fails to resolve the conflict between local detail sharpness and global structural coherence-especially in mixed urban-farmland scenes.These gaps not only reflect the lack of attention architectures tailored to remote sensing’s heterogeneous scenes but also ignore the reference value of “multi-feature collaboration” in cross-view intelligent person search–this study draws on the idea of multi-feature constraint in cross-view tasks to design a dual-attention discriminator, balancing local detail extraction and global structure perception. Wang et al. 3 (remote sensing cross-domain object detection) further confirms the effectiveness of multi-view adaptive feature alignment in addressing ground object scale diversity. Thus, SDGAN’s dual discriminator integrates this idea: D1 focuses on small-scale details and D2 on large-scale structures, making up for existing attention-based SR methods’ multi-scale adaptation deficiency. Beyond GANs, attention mechanisms, and residual blocks, advancements in other remote sensing deep learning tasks provide critical references for addressing SR’s core challenges. Context-aware landslide extraction models from aerial imagery emphasize cross-scale feature correlation between local terrain details and global topographic context, inspiring the Super-Dense Residual Block (SDRB)’s design of bidirectional feature interaction to reduce detail loss; multi-aspect collaborative salient object detectors integrating “localization, balance, and affinity” mechanisms balance detail and structure, guiding the dual discriminator’s division of labor (local detail focus vs. global structure constraint); multi-feature constrained cross-view intelligent person search methods fuse complementary features (appearance, posture, environment) to compensate for perspective-induced information loss, informing the Attention U-Net’s feature prioritization logic. These cross-task insights–context-aware interaction, multi-scale balance, and multi-feature constraint–are universal for handling remote sensing’s complex heterogeneity, and their integration into SDGAN strengthens the model’s rationality and innovation.Notably, Chen et al. 8 attempted to address resource-constrained scenarios (e.g., UAV-mounted processing) by designing adaptive residual blocks for remote sensing SR, but their model’s generalization across 30+ land-use categories is limited by single-scale feature extraction, and the computational efficiency still lags behind practical deployment requirements. Generator network with super-dense residual connections To reduce computational overhead, lightweight residual block designs such as SRNMSM 36 , 37 have been proposed, yet they still struggle with remote sensing image detail preservation. Traditional Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) generators employ direct or skip connections to facilitate information flow in deep architectures, mitigate gradient vanishing, and enhance model expressiveness. However, these approaches primarily depend on feature concatenation for fusion, Multiple dense residual block-based GANs have also been explored in medical image SR 38 , but their high complexity limits deployment in remote sensing scenarios. which inherently incurs high computational costs and memory overheads. Additionally, increased network depth exacerbates parameter optimization challenges, potentially leading to information loss and gradient instability. These limitations become particularly pronounced in high-resolution image processing–especially for remote sensing data–where conventional connection strategies often result in generated images with incomplete details or blurred edges. Super-dense residual block (SDRB) To address the multi-directional structural complexity, detail loss, and artifact issues of remote sensing images, the SDRB is proposed. Traditional 1D planar convolution-based blocks only enable unidirectional feature transmission, failing to capture complex spatial correlations and easily losing edge/texture details in deep networks. Traditional 1D planar convolution kernels in DenseNet only enable unidirectional feature transmission between adjacent layers, similar to a “single-lane path”. Feature information can only flow in a sequential manner, which not only results in low transmission efficiency but also easily leads to the loss of critical details such as edges and textures in deep networks. This limitation is particularly prominent in remote sensing images, where complex structural features (e.g., intersecting roads, irregular farmland boundaries) require multi-directional information interaction to be accurately captured. In contrast, 2D matrix convolution kernels arrange convolutional layers in a 2D matrix form and add diagonal connections, equivalent to upgrading the “single-lane path” to a “multi-directional transportation network”. On one hand, diagonal connections directly establish feature channels between non-adjacent layers, allowing basic texture information from shallow layers and high-level semantic information from deep layers to interact across layers–this cross-layer interaction significantly reduces information loss during feature propagation. On the other hand, the 2D matrix layout expands the receptive field of convolution kernels in a “grid-like” pattern, enabling simultaneous coverage of pixel correlations in horizontal, vertical, and diagonal directions. This design is highly compatible with the multi-directional structural features of remote sensing images (such as building edges, road orientations, and farmland textures), thereby accurately capturing details that are easily overlooked by traditional 1D planar convolution structures. As illustrated in Fig. 2 , the core innovation of the SDRB lies in its adoption of a 2D matrix configuration for convolutional kernels, which differs from the 1D planar layout of traditional DenseNet. This design introduces a large number of diagonal connections, doubling the density of feature paths compared to traditional dense blocks under the same number of convolutional layers. The expansion of short-range and long-range paths not only improves the efficiency of local feature extraction but also preserves rich texture details by enhancing multi-scale information fusion. Comparative experiments show that images reconstructed by BIPNet lack sufficient smoothness, while CBAM-GAN can focus on edge details and textures while ensuring output smoothness. However, the SDRB further optimizes the ability to represent feature details and overall outperforms other algorithms in terms of visual quality and structural integrity. These units fuse features extracted from parallel multi-path convolutions. Feature maps in each multi-path unit are not only shared across layers within the same unit via aggregation and dense connections but also serve as skip inputs for subsequent units. The function of the i-th unit can be expressed as: $$\begin{aligned} y_i= & H_c\bigl ([F_{i,0}(x_0), F_{i,1}(x_1), \dots , F_{i,n}(x_n)]\bigr ) \end{aligned}$$ 
 (1)
 $$\begin{aligned} \mathfrak {s}_{(i,n)}= & H_1\bigl ( H_c\bigl ( y_r, \mathfrak {s}_{(i-1,n)} \bigr ) \bigr ) \end{aligned}$$ 
 (2)
 In Eq. 1 , \(x_n\) denotes the input to the \(i\) -th path in the SDRB, while \(F_{(i,n)} (x_n)\) and \(y_i\) represent the feature maps of a single convolutional layer and the aggregated features across all layers in the \(i\) -th unit, respectively. Equation 2 defines \(\mathfrak {s}_{(i,n)}\) as the transformation output of the \(n\) -th path in the \(i\) -th unit, and \(\mathfrak {s}_{(i-1,n)}\) as the output of the \(n\) -th path in the \((i-1)\) -th unit. Functionally, a set of skip connections enhances the correlation between input and output feature maps. A transformation layer, denoted by \(H_1\) , is embedded into \(1{\times }1\) convolution kernels to reduce feature channels. By leveraging super-dense connections, features gain enhanced expressiveness through short or long range pathways. Notably, reducing filters and parameters enables a more compact and efficient architecture for remote sensing images, significantly lowering computational cost, memory pressure, and inference latency. To quantitatively verify the superiority of the SDRB in computational efficiency and reconstruction quality, Table 1 compares it with three typical existing core modules (DenseNet Dense Block, RDN (RDB), and PMSRN (MSDRB)) under the same experimental setup ( \(\times 4\) super-resolution on the UCMerced-LandUse dataset). The results show that the SDRB achieves the optimal balance between computational complexity and performance: in terms of efficiency indicators, its parameter size (0.82M) is 20.8% lower than that of RDN (RDB) (1.20M) and 20.0% lower than that of PMSRN (MSDRB) (1.12M), while its FLOPs (18.6G) are reduced by 26.5% and 18.4% compared to the two modules respectively, and the single-image inference time (0.87s) is the shortest among all compared modules, demonstrating significant advantages in lightweight design. Meanwhile, in terms of reconstruction quality, the edge detail PSNR of the SDRB (32.38 dB) is 3.7% higher than that of RDN (RDB) (31.22 dB) and 4.6% higher than that of PMSRN (MSDRB) (30.95 dB), which confirms that the SDRB can effectively improve the clarity of edge details while reducing computational overhead. This fully proves that the 2D matrix convolution kernel configuration and super-dense connection design of the SDRB can simultaneously optimize feature utilization efficiency and complexity control, overcoming the limitations of existing modules that often require a trade-off between performance and computational cost. Attention-based U-Net dual discriminator For remote sensing images’ multi-scale characteristics and scene heterogeneity, a dual Attention U-Net discriminator is designed. Traditional single-scale discriminators cannot balance local detail fidelity and global structural rationality, leading to artifacts or distorted macro-structures. Taking the lightweight U-Net as its foundation, Discriminator D incorporates a core attention mechanism module , which can adaptively learn the weight assignment of input feature maps during feature extraction and prioritize task-critical features,as shown in Fig. 4 . This structure consists of a downsampling encoder, an upsampling decoder, and multiple attention modules (where F, W, and H represent the number of output channels of the first convolutional layer, image height, and image width, respectively). The specific workflow is as follows: Initial features are extracted by convolutional layers. During the encoding phase, downsampling compresses the spatial dimension and increases depth complexity to capture global contextual information; During the decoding phase, upsampling gradually restores the feature maps to their original size to achieve detailed reconstruction; Skip connections fuse the feature maps of the encoder and decoder to ensure spatial coherence, ultimately enabling accurate discrimination of key features. Fig. 4 Full size image Architecture of the attention-based U-Net discriminator. The Attention Block (AB) is implemented via an Attention Gate (AG), and the Concentrate Block (CB) fuses feature maps from different network layers and scales. By combining local details from shallow layers with global context from deep layers, it enhances the ability to depict target structures in remote sensing images.As shown in Fig. 5 , the attention U-Net originally designed for 3D medical image segmentation is adapted to 2D images, significantly reducing computational costs: \(\textbf{x}^l\) denotes the input features to the U-Net, g serves as the gate control signal for feature processing, and \(F_{\text {int}}\) is a hyperparameter defining the number of output channels of the 1 \(\times\) 1 convolution in the AB. The input features \(\textbf{x}^l\) are adjusted and scaled using an attention coefficient \(\alpha\) to stabilize training, and Layer Normalization is applied for regularization. Dual discriminator For \(D_1\) (full-resolution image processing): Traditional single-scale discriminators need to focus on both global and local information simultaneously, leading to scattered computing resources and insufficient discrimination accuracy for small-scale details such as building edges and road textures (e.g., misclassifying pseudo-textures as real features). In contrast, \(D_1\) specializes in full-resolution images, and its Attention U-Net module can specifically enhance the weight assignment for pixel-level details. For instance, in remote sensing scenarios like ’airport runway stripes’ and ’farmland ridge boundaries’, \(D_1\) can more accurately distinguish between ’real textures’ and ’generated artifacts’, directly reducing the misjudgment rate at the detail level and improving the texture fidelity of super-resolved (SR) images. For \(D_2\) (2 \(\times\) downsampled image processing): The downsampling operation compresses redundant spatial information, allowing \(D_2\) to ignore local pixel noise and focus instead on macro-structures such as urban layouts and river network continuity. Traditional discriminators often suffer from broken global structures (e.g., missing river segments or disordered building arrangements) due to overemphasis on local details. However, by learning macro-scene rules, \(D_2\) can constrain the generator to avoid the problem of ‘realistic local details but incorrect global logic’–for example, ensuring the connectivity of road networks and the rationality of vegetation distribution, thereby enhancing the structural consistency of SR images. As illustrated in Fig. 6 , the SDGAN adopts a dual discriminator architecture to enable multi-scale discrimination, allowing the network to learn hierarchical features from coarse to fine. This design includes two identical Attention-Based U-Net discriminators: Discriminator \(D_1\) processes full-resolution images to capture fine-grained details, while Discriminator \(D_2\) operates on \(2{\times }\) downsampled images to emphasize global context and structural coherence. By combining \(D_1\) and \(D_2\) , the model balances local precision with global consistency, ensuring accurate reconstruction of both fine textures and large-scale patterns. Fig. 6 Full size image Architecture of the multi-scale discriminator. To address the generalization challenge in complex remote sensing scenes (e.g., diverse land-use types, uneven texture densities, and variable illumination), \(D_1\) and \(D_2\) achieve synergistic optimization through complementary feature learning and mutual constraint. Specifically, \(D_1\) focuses on local high-frequency details (e.g., building edges, road textures, and vegetation boundaries) via the Attention-Based U-Net, avoiding the loss of critical small-scale features; \(D_2\) reduces spatial redundancy through downsampling, prioritizing global structural coherence (e.g., urban layout, river network continuity, and agricultural distribution) to learn macro-scene rules. Their synergy is reflected in two aspects: first, the local details from \(D_1\) and global structures from \(D_2\) constrain each other during training– \(D_1\) prevents \(D_2\) from ignoring local authenticity due to overemphasizing global structure, while \(D_2\) guides \(D_1\) to avoid overfitting to local noise; second, the loss information of both discriminators is jointly fed back to the generator, prompting it to balance local precision and global consistency in super-resolution reconstruction. This dual-scale complementary learning enables the model to adapt to heterogeneous remote sensing scenarios, significantly improving generalization capability. In terms of constrained feedback: \(D_1\) feeds back the discrimination errors of local details (e.g., ’blurred edges’ and ’pseudo-textures’) to the generator, forcing it to optimize small-scale features. Meanwhile, \(D_2\) synchronously feeds back the discrimination errors of global structures (e.g., ’broken rivers’ and ’disordered building arrangements’), preventing the generator from neglecting the global context due to over-optimization of local details. This ’dual-error feedback’ reduces one-sided optimization of the generator, ensuring that SR images meet standards in both ’detail clarity’ and ’structural rationality’. In terms of generalization ability: Traditional single-scale discriminators have poor adaptability to ’complex scene heterogeneity’ (e.g., mixed scenes of ’dense buildings + open farmland’ in a single image), often resulting in ’satisfactory performance in one region but failure in another’. In the dual-scale design, \(D_1\) adapts to small-scale heterogeneous details (e.g., window textures in buildings and ridge intervals in farmland), while \(D_2\) adapts to large-scale heterogeneous structures (e.g., the boundary transition between buildings and farmland). Their combination enables the model to maintain stable performance across the 21-category UCMerced-LandUse dataset and 19-category WHU-RS19 dataset, enhancing cross-scene generalization.” The discriminator outputs a \(W{\times }H\) matrix where each element represents the probability of the corresponding pixel being real. To compute the total loss, the output is normalized via a sigmoid function \(\sigma (\cdot )\) followed by binary cross-entropy loss. Let \(\mathscr {C}\) denote the output matrix, define \(D = \sigma (\mathscr {C})\) as in Equation (3-3), where \(x_r\) is real data and \(x_f\) is fake data. $$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_D = \sum _{w=1}^{W} \sum _{h=1}^{H} \Bigl ( - \mathbb {E}_{x_r} \bigl [ \log \bigl (D(x_r, x_f)[w,h]\bigr ) \bigr ] - \mathbb {E}_{x_f} \bigl [ 1 - \log \bigl (D(x_f, x_r)[w,h]\bigr ) \bigr ] \Bigr ) \end{aligned}$$ 
 (3)
 The sigmoid function acts as the activation function, smoothly constraining the output range to avoid interference from extreme values, thereby stabilizing GAN training. The binary cross-entropy loss directly targets the real vs. fake classification task: minimizing it optimizes the discriminator’s classification accuracy. It also facilitates gradient propagation—generating larger gradients when predictions deviate significantly from ground truth to accelerate learning, and smaller gradients as predictions approach truth to aid convergence and prevent excessive oscillations. The proposed SDGAN framework consists of two core components: a generator ( \(\mathscr {G}\) ) and an Attention-Based U-Net discriminator ( \(\mathscr {D}\) ) for feature extraction. \(I_{\text {LR}}\) , \(I_{\text {SR}}\) , and \(I_{\text {HR}}\) denote the low-resolution input, super-resolution output, and ground-truth remote sensing image of SDGAN, respectively. To preserve precise details in remote sensing images and effectively address the challenges of high-frequency features and texture quality, this paper optimizes the loss function of SDGAN. A robust content loss function is constructed to force the generator \(\mathscr {G}\) to produce HR images similar to the ground truth using its given architecture, as shown in Eq. ( 4 ): $$\begin{aligned} \text {Loss}_c\bigl ( \theta _{\mathscr {G}} \bigr ) = \arg \min _{\theta _{\mathscr {G}}} \sum _{i=1}^{P} \rho \bigl ( I_{\text {HR},i} - I_{\text {SR},i} \bigr ) \end{aligned}$$ 
 (4)
 Here, \(\text {Loss}_c\) denotes the robust content loss, with \(\theta _{\mathscr {G}}\) representing the generator’s parameters, and \(I_{\text {SR},i}\) and \(I_{\text {HR},i}\) denoting features from the reconstructed and ground-truth images extracted by the Attention-Based U-Net discriminator, respectively. The Charbonnier penalty function \(\rho (x) = \sqrt{x^2 + \varepsilon ^2}\) — a variant of \(\ell _1\) loss — offers robustness, fidelity, and smoothness, making it suitable for remote sensing image reconstruction. Unlike pixel-wise loss (poor at handling small errors and neglecting texture quality) or \(\ell _2\) loss (overly smoothing textures by prioritizing large deviations), Charbonnier loss mitigates outliers through its formulation, enhancing detail preservation and robustness to input variations. This ensures focus on dominant content and structural features rather than noise. Here, \(\varepsilon\) is set to \(10^{-3}\) . Then, \(I_{\text {SR}}\) and \(I_{\text {HR}}\) are fed into \(D\) to determine whether they are real or fake. The discriminator is trained by minimizing the perceptual adversarial loss, which encourages the generator \(\mathscr {G}\) to produce reconstructed images \(I_{\text {SR}}\) that lie on the manifold of real images \(I_{\text {HR}}\) . The formulation for this process, as shown in Eq. ( 5 ), is as follows: $$\begin{aligned} \text {Loss}_{\text {ad}}\bigl ( \theta _{\mathscr {D}} \bigr ) = -\log \bigl ( D(I_{\text {HR}}) \bigr ) - \log \bigl ( 1 - D\bigl ( \mathscr {G}(I_{\text {LR}}) \bigr ) \bigr ) \end{aligned}$$ 
 (5)
 Here, \(\text {Loss}_{\text {ad}}\) denotes the perceptual adversarial loss, and \(\theta _{\mathscr {D}}\) represents the model parameters in the discriminator \(D\) . \(I_{\text {LR}}\) is the low-resolution input, \(\mathscr {G}(\cdot )\) denotes the generator network function, and \(D(\cdot )\) is the discriminator function. Given a high-resolution image \(I\) , \(D(I)\) represents the probability that \(I\) is a real high-resolution image rather than a fake super-resolution one. Therefore, the overall objective is given by Eq. ( 6 ) as follows: $$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}\bigl ( \theta _{\mathscr {G}}, \theta _{\mathscr {D}} \bigr ) = \text {Loss}_c\bigl ( \theta _{\mathscr {G}} \bigr ) + \lambda \, \text {Loss}_{\text {ad}}\bigl ( \theta _{\mathscr {G}}, \theta _{\mathscr {D}} \bigr ) \end{aligned}$$ 
 (6)
 Based on multiple experiments, this paper sets \(\lambda = 10^{-5}\) to balance the loss components. The generator \(\mathscr {G}\) and discriminator \(D\) are updated alternately, and the optimized generator is ultimately obtained when \(D\) no longer updates. Experiments Data To verify the proposed model, we trained and evaluated it on three publicly available remote sensing datasets: UCMerced-LandUse, WHU-RS19 (i.e., SIRI-WHU Dataset), and AID Dataset. All datasets are open for non-commercial academic research and can be accessed via their official repositories: UCMerced-LandUse ( https://aistudio.baidu.com/aistudio/datasetdetail/51628 ), WHU-RS19 ( https://github.com/201528014227051/WHU-RS19 ), and AID Dataset ( https://captain-whu.github.io/AID/ ). Performance was assessed using subjective visual inspection and objective metrics to validate the model’s effectiveness in diverse large-scale remote sensing scenes. The UCMerced LandUse dataset, a standard in remote sensing, includes 21 land-use classes; 15 samples per class (100 total) were used for training. WHU-RS19 (i.e., SIRI-WHU Dataset) comprises 19 high-resolution satellite categories from Google Earth, with 600 \(\times\) 600 RGB images. 15 samples per category were selected for training. AID contains 30 scene classes (about 220–420 images per class, 600 \(\times\) 600 pixels, about 10,000 total). For efficiency, 100 training images per class were used, sufficient to meet the training requirements. The AID dataset ( \(\approx 10,000\) images, 220–420 per category) would be overly large for full-scale training. With 2 NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs ( \(24\text {G VRAM}\) each), full data use would require batch size \(\le 16\) –prolonging iterations \(3\times\) (total training time \(> 120\) hours) and risking VRAM-induced interruptions. Selecting 100 images per category (3000 total) enables stable training at batch size \(= 48\) , balancing efficiency and hardware compatibility. AID’s uneven category sizes (e.g., 220 for “Airport”, 420 for “Forest”) would cause overfitting to large categories and poor generalization for small ones if used fully. Uniformly choosing 100 images per category eliminates such biases, ensuring balanced learning across 30 scenes. Controlled experiments (Table 2 ) show: increasing samples per category from 100 to 200 boosts PSNR by 0.12 dB/SSIM by 0.003 but raises training time by 68%; full data use only improves PSNR/SSIM by 0.08 dB/0.002. Diminishing marginal benefits confirm 100 images per category suffice–extra samples add little performance but high computational cost. Samples are chosen via “stratified random sampling”: first filtering out images with severe cloud cover/motion blur, then randomly selecting 100 from the rest. This ensures representation of category-specific features (e.g., docks/ships for “Port”, sand dunes for “Desert”) and avoids selection bias impacting generalization verification. Effectiveness and generalization analysis of SDGAN To comprehensively evaluate the perceptual quality of super-resolved (SR) remote sensing images, this study uses traditional objective metrics (PSNR, SSIM) with two auxiliary perceptual metrics– AG (Accuracy Grade) and NIQE (Natural Image Quality Evaluator) . AG is a reference-based metric (requiring real HR images) that quantifies “detail sharpness and structural integrity” via edge gradient consistency and texture similarity (range: 0–10; higher = better detail restoration, aligned with human perception). NIQE is a no-reference metric (no HR images needed) that assesses naturalness by comparing SR features to a natural image library (range: 0–10; lower = fewer artifacts, critical for land-use classification). AG and NIQE address PSNR/SSIM’s pixel-error bias (e.g., over-smoothed textures with high PSNR but poor visual quality), ensure edge integrity for ground object recognition, detect interpretation-interfering artifacts, and cover reference/no-reference scenarios, enhancing evaluation comprehensiveness. From Mean Opinion Score (MOS, a human perceptual metric), Real-ESRGAN and CBAM-GAN generate the highest-quality images (visually closest to original HR images), while bicubic interpolation performs worst. SRGAN and Real-ESRGAN have similar visual performance, but Real-ESRGAN outperforms in texture structure via a U-Net discriminator with instance normalization. For comprehensive and targeted comparisons, selected baselines cover key remote sensing SR technical routes: 1) SRCNN (classic CNN-based benchmark) – foundational for early remote sensing SR with pixel-wise loss; 2) SRGAN (pioneering GAN-based method) – first to introduce perceptual loss, shaping subsequent GAN-based SR; 3) Real-ESRGAN – optimized for real-world degradations (e.g., atmospheric interference), excelling in complex scene artifact suppression and widely used in practice; 4) BIPNet/CBAM-GAN (recent advanced methods) – BIPNet with superior PSNR/SSIM, CBAM-GAN enhancing remote sensing feature extraction via attention; 5) Bicubic interpolation – traditional non-deep learning baseline to highlight deep learning advantages. To further highlight SDGAN’s superiority in remote sensing super-resolution, this section discusses the limitations of representative GAN-based baselines (SRGAN and Real-ESRGAN) in complex scenarios, complementing quantitative comparisons. SRGAN, a pioneering GAN-based SR method, struggles with remote sensing-specific demands: it generates unrealistic artifacts in complex textures (e.g., interleaved pipelines, crisscrossing irrigation channels) due to inadequate multi-scale feature fusion, and its single-scale discriminator neglects global structural coherence (e.g., discontinuous river networks) while overemphasizing local details. Additionally, its natural image-optimized perceptual loss fails to preserve critical spectral and spatial fidelity for remote sensing tasks like land-use classification. Real-ESRGAN improves real-world degradation adaptation but still faces bottlenecks: it over-smooths fine-grained details (e.g., sparse vegetation, small communication towers) due to redundant multi-scale feature integration in RRDBs, and its generic U-Net discriminator lacks targeted prioritization of geographic key features (e.g., road edges, land-use boundaries). In multi-scale scenes (e.g., urban-suburban mixes), it fails to balance large-scale structure and small-detail restoration. In contrast, SDGAN addresses these limitations through targeted designs: SDRB’s 2D matrix convolution kernels suppress artifacts and preserve authentic textures; dual Attention-U-Net discriminators balance local details and global coherence; and Charbonnier-adversarial loss optimizes remote sensing-specific fidelity. Experiments verify SDGAN outperforms Real-ESRGAN by 1.89–3.51% in PSNR and 3.36–5.21% in SSIM, avoiding over-smoothing and artifacts in complex scenes. Conclusion To address challenges in existing remote sensing image super-resolution methods–including high complexity, resource consumption, poor generalization, and edge/detail degradation–this study proposes SDGAN, a lightweight framework combining Super-Dense Residual Blocks (SDRB) and dual Attention U-Net discriminators. The generator employs SDRB for enhanced feature extraction, mitigating information loss in dense connections to suppress artifacts. The discriminator integrates lightweight dual Attention U-Net branches to restore multi-scale texture details while reducing computational cost. Comparative experiments against SRGAN, Real-ESRGAN, BIPNet, and CBAM-GAN demonstrate SDGAN’s superior generalization across multi-category/multi-scale images, particularly in texture/fine-detail reconstruction. Computational complexity reductions of 21.72% and 14.67% on two datasets validate its efficiency, establishing SDGAN as a state-of-the-art solution for resource-constrained remote sensing super-resolution tasks. Future research directions can be expanded to further improve the framework’s practicality and versatility: First, exploring SDGAN’s adaptability to more complex remote sensing image degradations (e.g., Gaussian noise, salt-and-pepper noise, haze, and motion blur) by introducing degradation-specific feature enhancement modules and optimizing loss functions, to meet the needs of real-world scenarios with diverse image quality issues. Second, extending the model to handle multi-modal remote sensing data (e.g., hyperspectral, thermal infrared, and synthetic aperture radar (SAR) images) by adjusting the SDRB’s convolution kernel design and discriminator feature adaptation mechanisms, enabling cross-modal super-resolution reconstruction. Third, investigating the deployment of SDGAN on edge computing devices (e.g., UAV-mounted processors) by further compressing the model through techniques such as quantization and pruning, while maintaining reconstruction performance, to support real-time remote sensing data processing in field operations. Additionally, fusing SDGAN with downstream tasks (e.g., land-use classification and object detection) to build an end-to-end integrated framework, which can enhance the practical value of super-resolution results in remote sensing applications.
